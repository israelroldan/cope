## 1. Compoundway · `compoundway.app`

**The studio's stance applied to adulthood** — to the daily, unglamorous work of becoming better at your own life. Habits, money, skills, relationships, judgment, all framed not as tasks to check off but as quiet practices that compound over years.

The category is flooded — Streaks, Atomic Habits clones, finance apps, fitness apps — and almost all of them are engagement-mined: streaks, badges, gamification, *"you broke your chain!"* The Palú angle is the opposite. A Compoundway built right would feel closer to a daily journal than a tracker: minimal, dignified, time-respecting, almost meditative.

### What it could look like

- **A daily 3-minute compounding check-in.** Three or four small questions, no scoring, no streaks. *"What's one thing your 10-year-self would thank you for compounding today? What did you do yesterday that compounds? What did you avoid doing that decays?"* Stores quietly. Shows you back patterns over months, not minutes.
- **The compound horizon view.** Visualize how current behaviors compound at 1, 5, 10, 25 years across health, money, skills, relationships, taste, judgment. The killer feature isn't "you're at 12 days" — it's *"if you keep doing what you did last month for 10 years, here's the shape of your life."*
- **A practice library, not a habit list.** Practices have depth and seasons; habits are binary. "Reading 20 minutes daily" is a habit; *practicing reading* has stages, modes, life-chapters. The app meets you wherever you are in the practice.
- **Money as one channel of compounding, not its own walled garden.** Most finance apps wall money off. The studio's stance says money is just one axis among health, skills, relationships, taste. Treating it as continuous with the rest is the differentiation.

### Audience

Adults who've outgrown habit trackers but still want help. People who know "willpower app" doesn't work for them. Founders, knowledge workers, parents in their 30s/40s trying to be deliberate about what they're compounding rather than letting it happen by default.

### Why it fits

It's literally the studio's worldview applied to the user's own life. Practice over product. Kept progress over engagement. Intrinsic motivation over streaks. Taste over gamification.

### Discipline

Every UX decision is a test of the thesis here, because the gravitational pull toward the standard habit-tracker patterns (streaks, leaderboards, *"you've been away 3 days!"*) is enormous. Hold the line and Compoundway gets to be the rare app thoughtful people actually keep.

## 2. Curiosity Stars · `curiositystars.com`

StudyStars works because it respects the becoming of 10–17-year-olds. The same stance applied *earlier* — ages 6–10 — is genuinely a different product. Younger kids don't yet need programs and skill-building. They need *curiosity tended to* before school teaches them to suppress it. Curiosity Stars is the studio's stance applied to the un-curated wonder of being six.

### What it could look like

- **A weekly curiosity packet.** Every Monday, one curated micro-quest delivered to families. Could be digital (*"look up at the sky for ten minutes and write down three things you noticed"*), could be physical (a small monthly mailer with a seed packet, a magnifier, a paper template, a tiny notebook). Subscription model, low complexity, real *kept* artifacts that a child accumulates over years.
- **A "wonder library" for parents.** A quiet, curated bank of curiosity-prompts parents can pull from when a kid says "I'm bored." Anti-Pinterest, anti-craft-blog. Indexed by attention span, mood, available materials, location.
- **A 6–10 version of StudyStars's "keep what you build."** Instead of skills, what gets kept is *encounters*. A child's archive of what they noticed, wondered, what surprised them. Eventually graduates into a Library of Me account.
- **The bridge product across the kids portfolio.** If StudyStars is "kept practice for older kids" and Pimpi is "tiny apps to finish a thing," Curiosity Stars sits *upstream of both*: the practice of wondering, before the practice of doing or learning.

### Audience

Parents of 6–10-year-olds who want their kids' curiosity to survive the early-school years. Specifically: the same parents already in StudyStars's audience — younger siblings, future StudyStars graduates. The overlap is enormous, which makes the GTM cost approach zero.

### Why it fits

This is the most natural extension of an existing venture on the entire shortlist. Same operator, same brand DNA, same parent buyer, same family inbox. The risk-to-fit ratio is exceptionally good.

### Discipline

Don't slip into "early STEM" or "edutainment." Both categories are crowded and tonally wrong for the studio. The position is *protecting wonder*, not *accelerating learning*. The minute the copy starts saying "boost your child's curiosity," the brand is lost. Wonder is not a metric.

## 3. Strategyware + Junior Strategist · `strategyware.org` + `juniorstrategist.com`

**Strategic thinking as a lifelong craft — practiced from age 10 to senior strategist, in one studio worldview.** There is nothing good in either space. Adult strategy content is MBA-coded performance theater or LinkedIn framework-flexing; kids get nothing at all — strategic thinking, the meta-skill of how to navigate choices, is absent from school. Treating both as one studio practice with two products is the real architecture: same discipline, same stance, different points in the becoming arc.

The pair compounds the studio's worldview better than either alone. Strategyware says *"this craft never stops being worth practicing."* Junior Strategist says *"you can start practicing it earlier than anyone told you."* Together they make a position no one else holds: a studio that takes strategic thinking seriously as a lifelong practice rather than a credential to acquire or a deliverable to produce.

### What they could look like

**Strategyware** — for adults whose strategic judgment is still in motion.

- **Strategy katas.** Small, real-feeling situations dropped weekly. You write your move, your reasoning, your assumptions. Over months you have a body of judgments to look back at. Coding katas for the strategy brain.
- **A reflective decision journal.** Capture a real strategic decision you're making, your reasoning, your bet, your prediction. Months later the tool surfaces it back at you alongside what actually happened. Compounds judgment over years.
- **A small community of practice.** No LinkedIn vibes. People sharing katas, decision retrospectives, frameworks they're trying. The peer review part that schools and corporate strategy teams don't really give you.
- **Frameworks taught as ways of seeing, not templates.** Wardley, lean canvas, OKRs, theory of constraints — internalized via practice prompts, not filled-in PDFs.

**Junior Strategist** — for kids 9–14 developing strategic taste.

- **Weekly scenarios, not lessons.** Story-based situations where the kid practices one concept. *"Two friends both want the last cookie — what's a trade that leaves both okay with the outcome?" "Your team is losing the game — if you could only change one thing, which one and why?"* Concepts taught through cases, not vocabulary.
- **The strategist's notebook.** A growing, kept artifact — the kid's own decisions, scenarios worked through, things noticed, hypotheses tested. The "diploma" isn't a badge; it's the notebook itself. A parent can flip through it and see real reasoning developing over a year. This is the on-thesis answer to the certification question.
- **Concepts translated, not imported.** The IASP BOK and adult strategy frameworks are useful as a skeleton — but the translation work is the actual product. *"Environmental scanning"* becomes *"go for a walk and notice three things, and ask why each one is the way it is."* *"Stakeholders"* becomes *"who else cares about this decision besides you?"* *"What would have to be true?"* — that one works at any age unchanged.
- **Broader than strategy, taught through strategy.** Finance, operations, ethics, leadership, civics — all approachable as *"this is a trade-off problem," "this is a stakeholder problem," "this is a long-vs-short-term problem."* Gives the curricular breadth a parent would expect from a Junior MBA, without buying the brand's baggage.
- **A parent-companion mode.** Optional weekly conversation prompts a parent can use at dinner. Quiet, no homework energy. Makes the practice a family thing without turning it into school.

### Audience

**Strategyware:** founders, product leaders, in-house strategists, consultants — adults for whom strategy is a craft they want to deepen, not a deliverable they need to produce. Underserved, intrinsically motivated, perfectly inside the worldview once framed as becoming rather than output.

**Junior Strategist:** parents of 9–14-year-olds who already buy chess, debate, philosophy-for-children, *The Week Junior*, *Brilliant*. Thoughtful-ambitious parents who want their kid to develop *judgment*, not just stack credentials. Heavy overlap with the StudyStars buyer — same family inbox — so GTM cost is close to zero.

### Why the pair fits

Each product clears the filter on its own. Together they do something neither does alone: they *anchor* the studio's claim that becoming is a lifelong arc, not a phase. Same discipline, two ages, one worldview. There's also a real internal flywheel — content, frameworks, scenarios, and voice can move between the two products. An adult Strategyware essay can seed a kid-friendly scenario; a Junior Strategist case can return as an adult kata. The studio gets to do the thinking once and ship it twice, at different altitudes.

The `.org` on Strategyware stays right — positions it as a serious practice space, not a vendor. The pair also gives the studio a cleaner answer to *"what is Palú Labs for?"* — we build for people whose judgment is still in motion, at any age, in any craft. Strategyware + Junior Strategist makes that claim legible in a way no single venture can.

### Discipline

Three lines to hold:

**Resist enterprise positioning on Strategyware.** Once you chase the procurement buyer, the surface has to lie about what's underneath — and the surface is the product. Stay individual-buyer, stay intrinsic, stay quiet. Nothing on the site can read like McKinsey or HBR; the voice has to belong to the studio.

**Earned legibility, no hollow gamification.** The studio doesn't refuse credentials — it refuses *hollow* ones. The kid's notebook is itself a credential: a real, growing record of strategic reasoning a parent can show a school, a kid can put in a portfolio, that's legible to the world *because* the substance is in it. A certificate of completion that says "this child worked through 40 real scenarios and reflected on each in writing" is fine — even good — because it's backed by the work. What stays out: gamified mechanics divorced from substance — streaks, level-ups, generic badges, gold stars, anything that produces engagement without producing capability. **The principle: every credential the product issues is backed by work the kid actually did. The credential reflects the substance, or it doesn't exist.**

**Serve the future-leader aspiration; refuse the existing market's shape.** Parents who want their kid to become a leader, a founder, an executive, a change-maker — these are real, legitimate aspirations the thesis welcomes. The studio is not in the business of teaching parents that their ambitions for their kids are wrong. What we don't borrow is *how* that aspiration is currently served by the market: Shark Tank Jr. energy, business plan competitions for 11-year-olds, pitch-deck vocabulary as substitute for thinking, credentials as extraction, "future CEO" branding that sells a costume instead of a practice. Same audience the Junior MBA programs court — fundamentally different product underneath. The aspiration is welcomed at the door; the engagement-mining stays outside.

## 4. Swipetry · `swipetry.app`

The swipe gesture isn't engagement-mining by default — that's only true if the *intent* behind it is engagement. The same gesture with a different intent is **low-friction discovery**, which is exactly what you need when curiosity has gone flat.

The framing is *curiosity map / dopamine menu*: a curated catalog of small real things to try, surfaced through a near-zero-cognitive-load UI. There is no good app for *"I'm bored, show me something real to do."* That's a hole in the market specifically because filling it requires resisting the engagement-mining temptation that captures everyone who tries. Doing the opposite is the moat.

### What it could look like

- **For kids.** A curated deck of "try this today" cards: micro-crafts, mini-experiments, small adventures, kitchen things. Swipe through, pick one, finish it, keep what you make. Sits next to Pimpi as a complement: Pimpi helps you finish what you started; Swipetry helps you find what to start.
- **For adults.** A dopamine menu of small real-world things: call an old friend, take a 20-minute walk you've never taken, learn one knot, read a poem aloud, cook one new vegetable. Anti-burnout, anti-scroll. The opposite of TikTok wearing a Tinder costume.
- **For families.** A shared swipe session for the weekend. Parents and kids swipe together, land on one thing both want, go do it. Given the existing family audience across StudyStars / Pimpi / Kimbi, this is probably the variant to ship first.

### Audience

Anyone whose curiosity has gone flat. Bored kids and bored adults are the same user philosophically. Families looking for "what should we do this Saturday" are the wedge.

### Why it fits

Reframed correctly, Swipetry is the most *Pimpi-adjacent* idea on the list. Pimpi is "turn curiosity into action." Swipetry is "surface the curiosity in the first place." They're complements, not competitors. Both reject infinite scroll. Both make small things feel doable.

### Discipline

The design discipline is everything. No scoring, no streaks, no nagging notifications, no "discover more for premium." Every interaction has to feel like the app is on *your* side, not extracting from you. Nail that ethos and Swipetry isn't a cute app — it's a small cultural position. Lose the ethos for even one quarter chasing retention metrics and it's just another infinite swipe deck.

## 5. Oddly Serious · `oddlyserious.com`

This isn't a venture in the same shape as the others. It's the studio's *voice channel* — the place where the thesis is articulated out loud and where the studio's worldview gets to compound as written work. Calling it a "venture" is almost a category mistake; it's the studio's reading room. But it earns its place on the list because every other venture *needs* it to exist, for distribution to be congruent with values.

### What it could be

- **A small, considered newsletter.** Biweekly or monthly. Reflections on building tools for becoming, parenting in a thoughtful era, the indie software tradition, NL/Eindhoven life, the slow process of running a studio. Voice: quiet, pointed, occasionally funny. Tonally a cousin to Craig Mod's *Roden*, Robin Sloan's letters, 37signals's old blog.
- **A long-running essay archive.** Pieces that age well, hosted at oddlyserious.com itself. The site becomes a slow library, not a feed. Searchable, well-typeset, the kind of site people bookmark and re-read.
- **Origin stories for every venture.** Each Palú launch gets a piece on Oddly Serious explaining *why this one, why now, who it's for, what we're trying not to do.* Becomes the audit trail of the studio's thinking — useful for partners, future hires, and future you when memory blurs.
- **Occasional letters or short interviews.** Small conversations with other people doing quiet, thoughtful work. Not big-name interviews; real ones with practitioners. The kind that build the network the studio actually wants to be in.
- **(Optional, later) a slow podcast.** Same voice, audio. Once a month. No "subscribe rate review" energy. Defer this; newsletter first.

### Audience

The kind of person who would buy from Palú's other ventures. Thoughtful operators, considered parents, people fatigued by the loud internet. This is the audience that becomes the studio's customer for everything else, *because* they already trust the voice.

### Why it fits

Every venture in the portfolio needs a distribution channel congruent with its values. The fastest way to break the studio's brand is to acquire customers through the same channels (paid ads, growth hacks, viral loops) the products explicitly reject. Oddly Serious is the alternative — slow, organic, voice-driven distribution. It's also the receptacle for thinking that would otherwise stay inside your head.

### Discipline

Voice over volume. If you publish biweekly for two years and the body of work is *real*, the audience compounds and never leaves. If you publish weekly performatively, the brand erodes faster than any other venture could rebuild. The non-negotiable: nothing on Oddly Serious reads like LinkedIn or Medium thought-leadership. If a draft drifts that way, kill it.

**Honest note:** Oddly Serious is also the project most directly threatening to your time, because writing well is slow. If you take it on, commit to shipping the first 12 issues before judging it. If 12 feels like too much, defer the venture and write privately first — the worst version of Oddly Serious is the one that gets started, dies after 4 issues, and leaves a stale archive online.

## The filter, as a reminder

> Does this help someone — at whatever life stage, in whatever craft — turn curiosity into kept, tangible progress, with taste and care, on something nobody else is teaching them?

All six clear it. If a seventh idea ever appears that clears the same bar, it belongs on this list. If a tempting idea doesn't clear it, it doesn't.
