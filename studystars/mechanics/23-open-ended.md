# Open-Ended Text Response

> Learners compose free-form text in response to a prompt; Pimpi evaluates the response in character against a rubric and gives warm, specific feedback.

## Core Mechanic

The screen displays a prompt (e.g., "In your own words, explain why the dinosaurs went extinct") and a large text input field. The learner types their response, with a live character counter showing remaining allowance (e.g., "47 / 500 characters"). The learner can draft, revise, and submit.

Upon submission, **Pimpi evaluates the response**. This is NOT the "Explain to Pimpi" multi-turn reflection at the end of a Spark — this is a single-submission activity where Pimpi reads the answer and gives feedback in character. Pimpi is the evaluator here, but warmly: it celebrates what the kid got right, gently points out what's missing, and optionally shows the model answer.

Pimpi's feedback is specific and rubric-aware but never reads like a grade sheet. Instead of "Evidence: 8/10", Pimpi says "You mentioned the steam engine and coal — great! But what about the workers? There were a LOT of people moving to cities..." The rubric drives what Pimpi checks for, but the kid only sees Pimpi's conversational feedback.

The interaction is open-ended, forcing learners to generate and articulate their own thinking — the hallmark of Bloom Level 6 (Create).

## Why It Works

**Open-ended responses demand original synthesis (Bloom Level 6).** Learners can't select from options; they must generate their own answer. This forces deeper cognitive processing and reveals the true depth of their understanding.

**Rubric-based feedback is concrete and actionable.** Rather than a vague "good job" or "needs work," rubric-based feedback identifies specific strengths and areas for improvement. Learners understand what they did well and what to focus on next.

**Writing deepens learning.** The act of articulating ideas in writing solidifies memory and reveals gaps in understanding. Learners who struggle to write an explanation often discover they don't fully understand the concept—which is valuable feedback.

**Revision and iteration are possible.** Unlike timed tests, open-ended responses can be drafted, saved, and revised. This supports a growth mindset and realistic writing practices.

## Pimpi as Evaluator — How It Differs from "Explain to Pimpi"

| | **Open-Ended Activity (#23)** | **Explain to Pimpi (Spark Reflection)** |
|---|---|---|
| **When** | During the Spark sequence, like any other activity | At the end of the Spark, after all activities |
| **What kid does** | Writes a specific answer to a specific question | Explains the whole topic in their own words |
| **Turns** | Single submission → Pimpi responds once | Multi-turn conversation (5-10 turns) |
| **Pimpi's role** | **Evaluator** — checks the answer against a rubric, gives feedback | **Listener** — reacts, asks follow-ups, never evaluates |
| **Observer** | Not needed — Pimpi evaluates directly | Silent LLM that tracks comprehension and signals Pimpi |
| **Tone** | Warm but gives real feedback ("You got X right, but missed Y") | Purely curious and encouraging, never corrects |
| **Data produced** | Rubric score + Pimpi's feedback text | Comprehension snapshot for learner profile |

### Pimpi Evaluator Prompt Template

The `pimpiEvalPrompt` field in the contentSchema is a system prompt generated by the Spark generator. It gives Pimpi the rubric, the model answer, and personality rules for evaluating this specific question.

```
You are Pimpi, a friendly learning companion. A child (age {{AGE_GROUP}}) just answered a question about "{{TOPIC}}" and you need to give them feedback.

The question was:
"{{PROMPT}}"

A great answer would cover:
{{#each RUBRIC}}
- {{this.criterion}} (worth {{this.maxPoints}} points)
  {{#if this.exemplar}}Example: {{this.exemplar}}{{/if}}
{{/each}}

Model answer for reference (don't quote this to the kid):
"{{MODEL_ANSWER}}"

Your job:
1. Read their answer carefully
2. Check which rubric criteria they addressed
3. Give feedback IN CHARACTER — warm, specific, encouraging

Rules:
- Start with what they got RIGHT. Always celebrate something, even if the answer is weak.
- For missing criteria, hint rather than lecture: "Ooh, but what about [topic]? I feel like there's something important there..."
- For incorrect statements, gently redirect: "Hmm, I remember hearing something a bit different about that..."
- Keep it SHORT: 2-4 sentences max. This is a quick check-in, not a report card.
- NEVER show a numeric score. No "3 out of 5." Pimpi doesn't think in points.
- Use language appropriate for a {{AGE_GROUP}}-year-old.
- End with encouragement, always.
- If the answer is really good, be genuinely excited: "Wow, you nailed it!"
- If the answer is thin, be supportive: "Good start! Want to see how someone else answered it?" → then show the model answer.

Respond with JSON:
{
  "pimpiFeedback": "string — Pimpi's conversational feedback (shown to the kid)",
  "showModelAnswer": true/false,
  "rubricScores": [
    { "criterion": "string", "score": number, "maxPoints": number }
  ],
  "overallScore": 0.0-1.0
}

The kid sees ONLY pimpiFeedback (and the model answer if showModelAnswer is true). The rubricScores and overallScore are stored for analytics but never shown to the kid.
```

## Content Generation Spec

The AI must produce:
1. A **prompt** that is clear, specific, and appropriate to the age/topic.
2. A **modelAnswer** that exemplifies a high-quality response for the target age.
3. A **rubric** array with 2–4 criteria, each with maxPoints and optionally an exemplar.
4. **maxLength** specifying character limit based on age.
5. A **pimpiEvalPrompt** — the system prompt for Pimpi's evaluation (generated from the template above, filled with this activity's rubric and model answer).
6. Optionally, an **explanation** for human reviewers.

The prompt should be specific enough to guide learners but open enough to allow multiple valid approaches.

## UX Considerations

- **Large, accessible text area:** Minimum 200px height, font size 14px+. Adequate padding. Light background, dark text for contrast.
- **Character counter:** Display live counter (e.g., "124 / 500 characters") below the text area. Change color if the learner exceeds the limit.
- **Placeholder text:** Provide a hint about expected length and type (e.g., "Type a short paragraph explaining...").
- **Draft saving:** Auto-save the learner's draft periodically (every 30 seconds) to prevent data loss from crashes or accidental navigation.
- **Keyboard management:** On mobile, ensure the text area is positioned so the keyboard doesn't cover it. Use pan or expand-on-focus patterns.
- **Submit button:** Make it clear and obvious, but prevent accidental double-submission (disable after first click).
- **Feedback display:** Show rubric-based feedback in a clear, itemized format. Highlight strengths and growth areas separately. Include the model answer for reference.

## Age Adaptations

| Age Group | Adaptations |
|-----------|------------|
| 8–10 | Simple, concrete prompts requiring 1–2 sentences. Max 200 characters (roughly 30–40 words). Supportive framing ("Tell me about...", "Draw a picture with words..."). Rubric is minimal: 1–2 criteria, max 10 points total. Feedback is celebratory and encouraging, with the model answer shown as an example of a great response. Example: "Describe your favorite animal and why you like it." |
| 11–13 | Paragraph-length prompts requiring explanation or inference. Max 500 characters (roughly 75–100 words). Rubric has 2–3 criteria (e.g., "explains clearly", "gives evidence", "is organized"). Feedback is constructive, pointing out what the learner did well and one area to improve. Example: "Explain what the American Revolution was and why it happened." Model answer shows a well-structured paragraph. |
| 14–16 | Analytical or argumentative prompts. Max 1000 characters. Rubric has 3–4 criteria (thesis, evidence, reasoning, organization). Feedback is sophisticated, discussing nuance and encouraging deeper thinking. Example: "Analyze the role of women during the American Revolution. What challenges did they face and how did they contribute?" Model answer exemplifies strong analysis, interpretation, and evidence use. Can include optional follow-up prompt: "How would you develop this further?" |

## Lesson Placement

This mechanic works best in **Reflection** or **Extension** phases, after content has been introduced and practiced. Open-ended responses are not ideal for rapid formative assessment but are excellent for summative assessment, synthesis, and metacognition.

Can appear in **Deep Engagement** as a culminating activity that ties together multiple concepts.

Avoid in **Hook** or early **Post-Content** where objective feedback is more valuable.
